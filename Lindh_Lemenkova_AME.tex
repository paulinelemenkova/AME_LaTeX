\begin{abstract}
  \label{abstract}

	Measuring permeability, compressive strength and compactness of the stabilised soil is important for engineering construction in the coastal area of Östrand in the Bothnian Bay of the Baltic Sea with dominated clayey soils. To gain insights into the variations of permeability and uniaxial compressive strength (UCS) of soils stabilised by different ratio of binders, soil samples were used as a test material stabilised by different ratio of the stabilised agents and varying water content. The binder agents included cement (Basement CEM II / A-V, SS EN 197-1) and ground-granulated blast furnace slag (GGBS). The samples were collected from the coastal region of Timrå, Bothnian Bay. The aim was to estimate the compressive characteristics, permeability and compactness of soil by measuring Uniaxial Compressive Strength (UCS) for each of admixtures. Factorial experiment was sued to test water ratio and amount of binder as variables. Permeability tests were performed on the stabilised soil with varied stabilising agents in binder ratio $H_WL_B$ with ratio 70/30\%, 50/50\%, and 30/70\%. The highest level of permeability was achieved by 70/30\% ratio of cement/slag, while the lowest -- by 30/70\%. The Proctor compaction tests were performed on a mixture of ash and green liquor sludge in order to determine optimal moisture content at which soil becomes the most dense and achieves maximum dry density. The maximal dry density at 1,12 $g/cm^3$ was achieved by 38.75 \% of water in a binder. Shear strength and P-wave velocity were measured using ISO/TS17892-7 and visualised as a function of compressive strength. The results show varying permeability and UCS of soil stabilised by varying ratio of CEM II/GGBS agents. 
	
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction} 
\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{Background} 
The fundamental structure of soil includes its important morphometric parameters, such as grain size and shape, pores and arrangement of elements within the soil mass. In turn, these largely influence its physical and mechanical properties: strength, compactness, permeability, air-filled porosity and gas diffusivity \cite{Ball}. These parameters can be measured through a series of practical experiments that include measurement of strength, porosity, compactness and permeability using various geotechnical devices and using existing manuals on soil testing \cite{Day2001,Davis2008}. For example, because soil is a complex structure consisting of solid particles and voids filled with water and air, its compactness and porosity can be assessed using evaluation of water content and dry density related to a soil structure \cite{Hillel}. Correlations between soil hydraulic, physical and mechanical properties ca be found by quantifying the water content in pore structures, which is possible through estimating soil permeability \cite{BARBOSA2022115673}. Thus, through practical testing of soil parameters it becomes possible to evaluate its bearing capacity for engineering purposes. Engineerings testing of soil is crucial for safety of structures and infrastructure to avoid human losses and damages. Therefore, evaluating soil parameters has a final goal of assessment of its bearing capacity prior to infrastructure construction, e.g. roads, pavements, buildings. 

Often properties of soil should be improved before the earthworks, in order to increase its strength and bearing capacity. This is often a case for weak and compressible soils, such as silt and clays, usually located in coastal areas. The improvement of physical and mechanical properties and behaviour of soils is performed through stabilization and solidification (S/S) process. The procedure of soil stabilisation is determined, evaluated and reported in many previous civil engineering studies and earthwork tests \cite{Jauberthie,Lindh202102,ArabiWild,Lindh2004}. Thus, improvement of compression characteristics of clayey soil is required prior to infrastructure constructions (roads, pavements or buildings) where human lives might be affected in case of damages or cracks caused by weak soil capacity. 

The stabilisation of soil is performed by stabilising agents (or binders) added into the soil masses. The impact of adding binders on soil strength depends on a variety of factors. Among other, these include soil type. For example, properties of silt and clay can be significantly improved by adding Portland cement or slag into the soil mass. Another factor includes a curing time: in general, the compression of soil increases over time \cite{Lindh202102}. Yet another factor is the type and amount of binder used for stabilization which notably affects the quality of the stabilized soil. The most common stabilising agents are cement \cite{Rekik}, cementitious products, such as fly ash \cite{Tastan,Hasan}, cement kiln dust \cite{Solanki,Lindh2021a}, lime. The by-products of industrial processes or iron and steel-making, such as ground granulated blast furnace slag \cite{Mozejko} and sludge \cite{Ingunza,Sharif} are also used as binders. Besides the type of the stabilising agent, the process of soil stabilization includes practical estimation and determining the amount of admixtures mixed with the tested soil affects its compressive strength and microstructure \cite{Lindh2001,Ahmed}.

Soil compactness, permeability and compressive strength are important technical properties of the soil that should be measured during S/S techniques before the construction works \cite{LindhWinter,XU2021114774}. Extensive literature is available on the S/S of soil and measuring its properties including soil types, testing methods and reported results \cite{Anagnostopoulos,Kallenetal2016,Lemenkov2021a,HUANG2022115452}. Comparatively fewer studies have focused on soil compaction \cite{Kongkitkul,Leeetal2007} and the impact of changed binders in various ratio for stabilization, permeability and compressibility of soils \cite{;;;}. Soil compaction is commonly resorted to in order to improve the density of soil by by reducing the void space, or the amount of air, between the soil particles in a weak clay soil. 

Evaluation of soil compaction is related to measurement of shape indices of pores and solid phase elements along with the physical soil parameters \cite{Bryk}. Compacted clayey soils are one of the most frequently used materials in the construction of infrastructure due to their physical and mechanical properties, since compacted clays usually stay in an unsaturated condition \cite{Zou}. Proctor compaction test is commonly used to measure soil compaction, due to its advantages and robustness \cite{Wasman,Matteo,Boudlal}. Its applications are diverse in earthworks, geotechnical engineering and agricultural sciences. For instance, Proctor compaction test has long been used to assess safety and bearing capacity of roads, pavements and building foundations using evaluation of the heterogeneous subgrade \cite{Barden,Jibon}. Moreover, studies on agricultural and horticultural production and forest management used Proctor test to assess the compactness of soil that presents an increasingly challenging problem in agriculture \cite{ARAGON2000197,Bayat,Nhantumbo,ALAOUI20111}. 

Besides Proctor test, existing standards methods are developed to perform compaction tests in a laboratory used to determine the relationship between molding water content and dry unit weight of soils \cite{ASTM2007,ASTM2009}. Other examples of testing soil compaction include, for instance, using gyratory compactor \cite{Leeetal2007}, vibrating compaction and modified Proctor compaction on the stress-strain characteristics and shear strength of graded crushed stone \cite{Long}. Yet another study used Time Domain Reflectrometry (TDR) and oedometer to measure soil moisture through assessment of texture and macropores after vertical compression of a compacted soil mass \cite{Alaoui}. The AASHTO prediction method is tested for measuring density and optimum moisture content of a soil sample \cite{Livneh}. 

\subsection{Study objectives} 
The present study examines the physical and mechanical properties of soil, including compression behaviour, permeability and compactness of cement/slag-stabilized soil samples within the frameworks of silt loamy soils collected in northern Sweden, Bothnian Bay of the Baltic Sea. Specifically, the study was a part of a project run by on behalf of the SCA Biorefinery Östrand AB having an ultimate goal to test the bearing capacity of ground prepared for planned construction works in the coastal area of Timrå municipality. 

The specific objectives of this study include the following tasks: 

\begin{enumerate}
   \item to analyse grain size distribution;
   \item to determine values of the compressive strength of soil stabilised by various combination of binder/water ratios (low/high) and stabilising agents (cement/slag) in various ratios (70/30\%, 50/50\%, 30/70\%); 
  \item to test soil permeability through a series of duplicate tests for different soil and binder proportions ($H_WL_B,L_WH_B,L_WEL_B$) with cement/slag for high-low soil and binder ratios: 70/30\%, 50/50\%, and 30/70\%;
  \item to perform Proctor compaction test and graphically visualise soil curves in oder to evaluate the degree of maximum moisture content at which soil is the most dense and achieves maximum dry density;
  \item to evaluate Uniaxial Compressive Strength (UCS) tests for each mixture of samples stabilised by different binder ratios;
  \item to find the correlation between the P-waves velocities and compressive strength of soil samples with various binder content (120 and 150 $kg/m^3$), measured by UCS;
  \item to observe the variability in values of the P-wave velocity for soil materials taken from the A4 and B4 sample sites and stabilised by various amount of binder, and visualise the difference on histograms;
  \item to compare the results of P-wave velocity changing with compressive strength for all soil samples from various phases of the experiment and binder ratios with other available data, to visualise these results on a graph.
\end{enumerate}

This study is focused on the evaluation of changing properties of the stabilised soils via two binders as stabilising agents: cement (CEM II / A-V) and ground granulated blast furnace slag (GGBFS). Studies in literature mostly focus upon the selected types of binder used as a stabilising agent in a given proportions, for instance, lime \cite{Rao,Mukhtar,Swaidani}, fly ash \cite{Phetchuay}. Other studies reported tests on various types of soil, such as frozen soils \cite{Lemenkov2021b,Lemenkov2021c}, sand soil \cite{Robertson}, clay or silt \cite{Komal}. Some reports discuss the technical issues of data processing and visualisation \cite{Kaellenetal2014,Lemenkov2021c}. In contrast, this study evaluated the effect of varying proportions of ratio of the stabilising agents assesses as a two-factor experiment. Besides, we analysed the ratio of binder/water content in a soil mixture in order to define the optimal recipe and admixture proportions for increase of strength, and evaluated the effects of green liquor sludge and ash for soil compaction.

\section{Methodology}
Evaluation of the soil properties is based on various criteria which include regional type of soils, ISO standard requirements for workflow, amount and availability of dredged samples. The samples were collected from the site prepared for construction of the SCA Biorefinery Östrand AB in coastal region of Timrå municipality, Bothnian Bay, Figure \ref{fig:01}. Using the materials and technical guidance enables to determine the type and amount of binder that should be used for a particular case as a the stabilising agent. Methodology included a series of soil tests performed in the Swedish Geotechnical Institute (SGI). The documents of the Swedish Institute for Standards were sued as guidance for the workflow \cite{SIS2017,SIS2018,SIS2019}.

\subsection{Phase 1: Experimental design}
The stabilisation of soil followed the existing general procedure requirements developed by the Swedish Institute for Standards \cite{SIS2018}. To optimise the experimental design, a special methodology was used based on the statistical experimental planning and Swedish Geotechnical Institute. An experimental design of mixture procedure was used for the selection of optimal ratio of the stabilising agent (cement and slag in tested varied amounts) and water. The mixture procedure consisted of preparation of various ratios of water and soil with experimentally varying quantities and/or types of stabilising agents (cement and slag) and testing the strength, compactness and permeability of the resulting mixtures.

In the experimental design, three different binder combinations were evaluated with changed ratio, Figure \ref{fig:02a}. The ratio between the stabilising agents (cement and slag) in the three selected recipes was 70/30\%, 50/50\% and 30/70\%, respectively. A series of tests has been performed on these three mixtures to determine the compaction characteristics and permeability of soil samples and to measure the Uniaxial Compressive Strength (UCS). The experiment included the factorial experiment with varying water ratio and amount of binder. In addition, the ultrasonic P-wave tests were conducted using the existing methodology \cite{Lindh202102} to determine the strength of soil and estimate its suitability for construction works. 

In addition to binder combinations, the effect of a varied water ratio was tested together with different quantities of the stabilising agents (cement and slag) in various proportions in Phase 1 of the experiment. Phase 1 resulted in 30\% cement and 70\% slag constituting appropriate proportions at both 120 and 150 kg binder volume per cubic meter of dredged material. The experimental design with simultaneous evaluation of mixture proportions, binder quantities and water quotas helps to produce information that can be used to control the process in the field. the dredged masses are obtained because the water quotas are expected to vary.  

The combination of a high water ratio ($H_W$) and a low amount of binder ($L_B$) constitutes a “worst case scenario” as the material risks having a higher permeability and lower technical strength. The scenario will henceforth be referred to as "high water low binder" ($H_WL_B$). The opposite, low water ratio and high amount of binder gives “the best case, ie a material with low permeability and high technical strength. This combination is further referred to in the text as "low water high binder" ($L_WH_B$).

\subsection{Phase 2: Experimental design}

After the initial evaluation of Phase 1, a coarse selection of suitable recipes was obtained and further optimised in Phase 2. In Phase 2, an additional amount of binder was added: 100 kg of binder per $m^3$ of the dredged material. The reduction was based on the established requirements with regard to strength and permeability of the stabilised soil with an amount of binder of $120 kg/m^3$ of dredged material. The goal of Phase 2 was to optimise the cost of the recipe without losing technical / environmental quality of soil. Cost optimisation of the recipe also provides the optimisation of the reduced carbon, as the amount of binder affects the environment. The performed test combinations in Phase 2 are presented in Figure \ref{fig:03}.

Graph in Figure \ref{fig:03} shows the experimental setups of Phase 1 and 2. Phase 1 is indicated by the blue dots and Phase 2 by - red dots, respectively. Blue circle shows where Phase 1 and 2 overlap. High and low water ratios in these experiments consisted of 190 respectively 139\%. High, low and extra low amount of binder consisted of 150, 120 respectively, for $100 kg/m^3$. Compared with the experimental setup 1, the minimum amount of binder has been reduced to $100 kg/m^3$ of the dredged material. 

\subsection{Phase 3: Experimental design}

Phase 3 refers to the scaled-up experiment. Here the testing included adding 150 kg of binder. The notable results from the Phase 3 include the highest values of P-waves, because of the maximal amount of binder used in this part of the experiment, which contributed to the increase of strength of soil masses. The Constant Rate of Strain Cell (CRS) tests were not applied, because the material was estimated to be too solid to obtain any reasonable results. In case of softer soil contect it could be applied in order to measure the magnitude and rate of consolidation of saturated cohesive soils by continuous controlled strain axial compression, which was not the case in this study.

\subsection{Soil permeability}
As in the lab study, measurement of soil permeability was performed using the existing methodology of Swedish Institute for Standards \cite{SIS2019}. The workflow included a series of tests according to the cell pressure permeate method (SGI method, edition 15). \subsection{Proctor compaction test}
The Proctor compaction test was performed on a mixture of ash and green liquor sludge in order to determine optimal moisture content at which soil becomes the most dense and achieves maximum dry density. The Proctor test was selected because it enables a standardised approach to evaluate compaction of soil after stabilisation and changed content of structure within the soil masses. The mutual ratio was 50/50 calculated on the dry material. The Proctor tests are performed according to SS027109. The optimal water ratio is about 39\% to achieve the best packing of the material, i.e. the highest dry density, see Figure \ref{fig:08}. The maximal dry density at 1,12 $g/cm^3$ was achieved by 38.75 \% of water in a binder. 

The experiments were carried out to assess the possibilities of using green liquor sludge and ash from SCA's plant as landfill. The experiments performed were density determination according to heavy laboratory packing (modified Proctor test). This assessment was based on performed Proctor tests on current material. However, the result depends on the composition of the material and can vary with the degree of carbonation of the ash and the water ratio of the green liquor sludge.

\end{figure}

The relationship of water content in a binder vs. dry density of soil is presented in Figure \ref{fig:08}. The data were tested using Least Square method, Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) algorithm. The results of the test are presented in Figure \ref{fig:08} (Proctor compaction test) 
as an example of the soil compaction vs water content relationship obtained for the tested soil samples. 

The changes in the maximal dry density depending on water content in a binder indicated that wetness of soil processed by green liquor sludge and ash could be used to predict variation of compactness with changed ratio of water in the diapason of 30\%-65\% (Figure \ref{fig:08}). The maximal peak of dry density was achieved by water content of 38-39\% in the soil masses. After that the soil dry density decreased exponentially along with the increase of water content in the soil mass until 65\% (that is, a maximally measured value). Hence, the results of the Proctor tests demonstrated that dry density in soil is sensitive to changes in soil wetness.

\subsection{Measurement of strength and P-wave velocity}

The velocity of propagation of the ultrasonic P-waves in a soil mass is largely influenced by the mechanical, microstructural and elastic properties of soil. Therefore, solving the inverse problem, measuring velocities of P-waves can indicate the properties of soil, which are needed in order to assess the bearing capacity of ground. Such testing was performed by using ultrasonic P-waves to measure soil strength after stabilisation. The use of P-waves is a modern non-destructive aimed to analyse strength of the stabilised soils. Using non-destructive geophysical approaches enables to detect early micro damages in concrete and other building materials \cite{Rydenetal2016}. Non-destructive methods provide a rapid, robust and reliable approach to analyse the stabilized soils, alternative to the existing traditional methods. 

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{Fig_09}
  \caption{Uniaxial Compressive Strength (UCS) tests for each mixture}
  \label{fig:09}
\end{figure}

The specimens had a diameter of ca. 50 mm and a height of ca. 100 mm. The strength is determined according to ISO / TS 17892-7 \cite{SIS2017} according to the rules of the Swedish Institute for Standards. The deformation rate was about 1\% / min which gives an approximate speed of 1 mm/min at this specimen size. The results from the UCS tests are presented in Figure 1. The shear strength of the test specimens is obtained by multiplying the compressive strength by 0.5. All recipes meet the requirements for a minimum compressive strength of 280 kPa, compared with the laboratory study. 

This study included developing of mixture design of binders in various proportions (30/70\%, 50/50\% and 70/30\% of cement/slag composition) and quality control procedures which were evaluated during the series of soils testing. Properties and behaviour of the S/S soils shown to be dependent on binder content as stabilising agents. Therefore, the experiments have been performed for each recipe and the values of the compressibility and permeability were recorded for each binder combination. Besides, the Uniaxial Compressive Strength (UCS) and P-wave velocities were recorded for materials taken from the sample sites A4 and B4 with 120 and 150 kg of binder per $m^3$ of the dredged material.

The highest values of P-waves were recorded in case of Phase 3, where experiment included 150 kg of binder (red stars in the graph). The lowest values were recorded for the Phase 2 of the experiment, where 100 kg of binders were tested. In Phase 1 two levels of binder quantity per $m^3$ of the dredged material were tested: 150 kg and 120 kg, see red squares in the graph of Figure \ref{fig:11}.

Figure \ref{fig:11} shows the results of the tests on P-wave velocity visualised as a function of compressive strength and admixture of binders in various ratios. Strength shows a complex characteristics of the soil largely influenced by the structure of the material including a variety of components, such as voids, pores, imperfections, and specific local defects of the soil mass. Therefore, the relationship between the compressive strength and velocity of P-wave propagation shows a curve rather than direct straight line, representing their relationship. Nevertheless, in general higher strength is associated with higher P-wave velocity, as seen in Figure \ref{fig:11}. 

In general, the P-wave velocity increased with strength of the soils. \section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

A series of tests were performed to determine the properties of the stabilized soils collected from the selected sampling sites in coastal area of Timrå municipality, Bothnian Bay, Baltic Sea, northern Sweden. The collected soil samples were obtained from various tests of the Östrand AB (Figure \ref{fig:01}). Samples were processed, prepared and stabilized in the laboratory of SGI, Gothenburg. Measurements of the physical-mechanical properties of soil was performed according to the technical standards of SIS. The study aimed to assess the effects of various binder combinations on the compressive strength, compactness and permeability of soils. Soil samples were stabilised by the two agents: cement (Basement CEM II / A-V) and slag (GGBFS). The amount of binders were changed to evaluate optimal ratio of binder and water for soil stabilisation. 

The velocities of the ultrasonic P-wave propagation in soil masses were tested to evaluate the strength of the stabilised soil samples. P-wave velocities were determined on specimens of a dredged silt loamy soil stabilized with mixtures of two agents (CEM II / A-V and GGBS) following technical standards SGI and workflow requirements of SIS for testing procedures. A grain distribution analysis was performed on the aggregated samples. Compression strength, compactness and permeability of soil were also determined using UCS tests, SGI method for testing permeability (edition 15) and modified Proctor compaction test, respectively. The compaction properties of soil in tested specimens, identified using modified Proctor compaction tests, demonstrated the relationship between the water ratio in the pores of soil and dry density, which was demonstrated on statistical graphs.

A comparison of the UCS in soil samples stabilised by various proportions of binder was performed. Measurements of the ultrasonic P-waves velocity  demonstrated to be a fast, robust and reliable non-destructive method of evaluating soil strength. Compared to the traditional destructive methods, applied geophysical testing methods can applied used to measure physical and mechanical characteristics of the stabilized soil samples in a more flexible and simple way. Since measuring P-wave velocity is a non-destructive geophysical approach, it has the advantage of test replication. Thus, the repeatability of P-wave testing reduced the number of tests within the set of experiments and optimises workflow. In this way, mixture design can be tested through adding of binders in various proportions, to determine properties and bearing capacity of the stabilized soil. Considering these advantages, the P-waves velocities were measured to evaluate the soil strength. 

Changes in ultrasonic P-waves velocity were evaluated for each type of the stabilising agent and demonstrated in graphs showing P-waves and UCS of the samples stabilised by binders in various ratio (Figure \ref{fig:11}). The quality of soils stabilized by cement/slag agents was assessed using methods similar to those for compacted soils. The P-wave velocities of the stabilized soil samples demonstrated the general increase along with increasing strength of the samples over time of curing. Herewith, higher velocities were recorded for the soils stabilised by binder proportion cement/slag as 30/70\%, while lower velocities were obtained for soils stabilised by binder proportion cement/slag as 70/30\%, respectively. 

The velocity of P-waves increased with increasing strength of the soils and time of curing. The increase in P-wave velocity with strength was more pronounced for the case of B4 testing where $150 kg/m^3$ of binder was used for soil samples compared to the test A4 with 120 kg of binder, which demonstrated the direct effects of binder admixture on the overall increase of soil strength. The transmission of the P-wave occurs along the fastest path in the structure of the soil mass, which is directly related to the strength and stiffness of the soil mass stabilised by the agents (cement/slag). Hence, samples of the stabilised soil with $H_BL_W$ content demonstrated higher strength and increased velocity of P-waves compared to the samples with $H_WL_B$  content.

The compressibility and strength of clayey soil are a basic frame for interpreting their natural characteristics and suitability for construction. The physical and mechanical properties of soils depend on the inherent natural soil structure, that is, fabric and bonding characteristics \cite{Burland}. However, after stabilisation, these properties may be improved towards a higher strength and compressibility which increases bearing capacity of soil required for construction works. Stabilising binders (cement and slag) react with water and show a bonding effect that produced an increase in compressive strength of the stabilised soil samples. In such a way, the stiffness and durability of the soil-binder system is achieved, because the initial natural voids and pores of the raw soil structure are filled by the stabilising agents which increase strength of the stabilised soil, which was demonstrated in the obtained results. 

Admixture of stabilising agents, such as cement and slag in various proportions, demonstrated increase in soil strength. Related to the industrial goals, stabilisation of soil leads to the overall increase of bearing capacity of the soil structure, which was demonstrated in the performed experiments. A case study of this work included a coastal area of Östrand in the Bothnian Bay of the Baltic Sea, northern Sweden, where engineering constructions are planned. The dominated soil type of the dredged materials included silt loams. The results of the study can be considered in similar works with clayey soil evaluated for construction works. Future research can include further experiments on changing binder ratio and stabilising agents and to develop criteria of binder/water proportions specifically for S/S treatment of clayey soils collected in the coastal areas of northern Sweden.
