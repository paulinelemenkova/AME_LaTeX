\section{Methodology}
\subsection{Experimental design Phase 1}
To optimize the experimental design, a special methodology was used based on statistical experimental planning (Lindh and Åberg, 2017). In the experimental design, three different binder combinations were evaluated, Figure 1. The ratio between cement and slag in the three selected recipes was 70/30, 50/50 and 30/70 respectively.

In addition to the binder combinations, the effect of a varied water ratio was tested together with different binder quantities in phase 1. The experimental design with simultaneous evaluation of mixture proportions, binder quantities and water quotas helps to produce information that can be used to control the process in the field. the dredged masses are obtained because the water quotas are expected to vary.  

The combination of a high water ratio (HW) and a low amount of binder (LB) constitutes a “worst case scenario” as the material risks having a higher permeability and lower technical strength. The scenario will henceforth be referred to as "high water low binder" (HWLB). The opposite, low water ratio and high amount of binder gives “the best case, ie a material with low permeability and high technical strength. This combination is further referred to in the text as "low water high binder" (LWHB).

In Figure 4, the best and worst combination is marked in a matrix based on factor experiments (ie two variables that are tested at two levels, in the figure mentioned as "high" and "low"). High and low water ratios in these experiments consisted of 190\% and 139\%, respectively. High and low amount of binder consisted of 120 and 150 kg binder per $m^3$ of the mud mass, respectively.

\subsection{Experimental design Phase 2}

After an initial evaluation of phase 1, a coarse selection of suitable recipes was obtained which was further optimized in phase 2. Phase 1 resulted in 30\% cement and 70\% slag constituting appropriate proportions at both 120 and 150 kg binder volume per cubic meter of dredged material. In phase 2, an additional amount of binder was added: 100 kg of binder per cubic meter of dredged material. The reduction was based on the established requirements with regard to strength and permeability being met with a binder with an amount of binder of 120 kg per cubic meter of dredged material. The goal of phase 2 was thus to be able to optimize the cost of the recipe without losing technical / environmental technical quality. Cost optimization of the recipe also provides optimization of reduced carbon footprint as e.g. the amount of binder is driving this type of environmental impact. The performed test combinations in phase 2 are reported in Figure 5.

Graph in Figure \ref{fig:05} shows the experimental setups Phase 1 and 2. Phase 1 is indicated by blue dots and phase 2 by red dots. Blue circle shows where Phase 1 and 2 overlap. High and low water ratios in these experiments consisted of 190 resp. 139\%. High, low and extra low amount of binder consisted of 150, 120 resp. 100 kg / m3. Compared with experimental setup 1, the minimum amount of binder has been reduced to 100 kg per cubic meter of dredged material. 

Furthermore, experiments were carried out to assess the possibilities of using green liquor sludge and ash from SCA's plant as landfill. The experiments performed were density determination according to heavy laboratory packing (modified Proctor). CRS trials were rejected as the material was judged to be too solid to obtain any reasonable results. This assessment was based on performed Proctor tests on current material (see Appendix 1). However, the result depends on the composition of the material and can vary with the degree of carbonation of the ash and the water ratio of the green liquor sludge.

\subsection{Soil permeability}
As in the lab study, measurement of soil permeability was performed as a series of tests according to the cell pressure permeate method (SGI method, edition 15). Within each group, the permeability decreases with increasing amount of binder, see Figure 1. The test is performed with double samples and the spread within each group seems to decrease with increasing amount of binder. 

The permeability tests were performed according to the cell pressure permeate method (SGI method edition 15). The specimens tested in phase 1 came from the worst-case scenario, ie high water ratio and low amount of binder. Despite this, the results showed low to very low permeability, see Figure 23. Samples with an increased slag content gave a denser material. However, the quality requirement of 10-8 m/s was only achieved with 30\% of cement and 70\% of slag. Only samples from the $H_WL_B$ series were included in the experiments.

In laboratory Phase 2, eight permeability tests were performed. The recipe consisted of 30\% cement and 70\% slag at two different amounts of binder and two different water quotas. The results are shown in Table 6. The quality requirement of 10-8 m / s was achieved for low water ratio and low binder quantity (sample 1), high water ratio and high binder quantity (sample 4) and low / high water ratio and high binder quantity (samples 6 and 8). 

The response area from the analysis is reported in Figure \ref{fig:xx2b} (2D and 3D, respectively). In the yellow-red areas, a significant interaction is shown between a change in the amount of binder and the water ratio. The interaction results in a sharp deterioration in technical and environmental technical quality through increased permeability from about 10-7 to 10-8 m/s. The strong interaction shows the importance of working with well-thought-out recipe optimisation at the lab level if the goal is to be able to control the mixing process in the field so that set quality requirements are achieved and project uncertainties are reduced. A robust recipe must contain 120 kg of binder per cubic meter of dredged material to provide sufficiently low permeability at several different water quotas.

\subsection{Proctor compaction test}
Proctor tests were performed on a mixture of ash and green liquor sludge. The mutual ratio was 50/50 calculated on dry material. The Proctor tests are performed according to SS027109. The optimal water ratio is about 39\% to achieve the best packing of the material, ie the highest density, see Figure \ref{fig:26}.

\subsection{Measurement of strength and P and flex wave velocity}

The specimens have a diameter of about 50 mm and a height of about 100 mm. The strength is determined according to ISO / TS 17892-7 with a deformation rate of about 1\% / min which gives an approximate speed of 1 mm / min at this specimen size. Results from the uniaxial compression tests are presented in Figure 1. The shear strength of the test specimens is obtained by multiplying the compressive strength by 0.5. All recipes meet the requirements for a minimum compressive strength of 280 kPa, compared with the laboratory study. 

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{Fig_30}
  \caption{Uniaxial Compressive Strength (UCS) tests for each mixture}
  \label{fig:30}
\end{figure}

The results of the uniaxial pressure tests for each mixture are shown in Figure \ref{fig:30}. The result must be multiplied by 0.5 to obtain shear strength. For material A4 with a water ratio of about 135\%, an increase in the amount of binder means a doubling in strength. This effect is not visible for material B4 with a water ratio of about 69\%. The different amounts of binder are shown in the legend. 

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{Fig_31}
  \caption{Histogram showing the P-wave velocity for materials A4 and B4 with 120 and 150 kg of binder per cubic meter of dredged material.}
  \label{fig:31}
\end{figure}

Figure \ref{fig:31} shows the P-wave velocity of the specimens. Similar to the results of the uniaxial pressure tests, the scattering increases with increasing P-wave velocity. However, the increase in spread is lower and the overlap between B4\_120 and B4\_150 is considerably lower. 

A certain difference between the laboratory results and the scaled-up experiments can be seen in Figure \ref{fig:32}. The figure shows results from the different phases. Phase 1 (Ph1) was the initial laboratory experiments and phase 2 (Ph2) was the optimization phase to find a lower limit for the amount of binder. However, the difference in the variation is based on experiences from other projects, e.g. Arendal 2, Gothenburg and Kolkajen, Stockholm. 

 Figure \ref{fig:32} shows the results of the tests on P-wave velocity visualised as a function of compressive strength. Ph1 is the first lab phase, Ph 2 is the supplement with a lower binder content and Ph 3 refers to the scaled-up experiment. The results show similar patterns for the different phases. However, a certain displacement can be discerned, i.e. slightly lower strength at the same P-wave velocity for the scaled-up experiment. The results are within the expected variation based on comparable surveys.
