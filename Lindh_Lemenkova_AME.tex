\begin{abstract}
  \label{abstract}

	Measuring permeability, compressive strength and compactness of the stabilised soil is important for engineering construction in the coastal area of Östrand in the Bothnian Bay of the Baltic Sea with dominated clayey soils. To gain insights into the variations of permeability and uniaxial compressive strength (UCS) of soils stabilised by different ratio of binders, soil samples were used as a test material stabilised by different ratio of the stabilised agents and varying water content. The binder agents included cement (Basement CEM II / A-V, SS EN 197-1) and ground-granulated blast furnace slag (GGBS). The samples were collected from the coastal region of Timrå, Bothnian Bay. The aim was to estimate the compressive characteristics, permeability and compactness of soil by measuring Uniaxial Compressive Strength (UCS) for each of admixtures. Factorial experiment was sued to test water ratio and amount of binder as variables. Permeability tests were performed on the stabilised soil with varied stabilising agents in binder ratio $H_WL_B$ with ratio 70/30\%, 50/50\%, and 30/70\%. The highest level of permeability was achieved by 70/30\% ratio of cement/slag, while the lowest -- by 30/70\%. The Proctor compaction tests were performed on a mixture of ash and green liquor sludge in order to determine optimal moisture content at which soil becomes the most dense and achieves maximum dry density. The maximal dry density at 1,12 $g/cm^3$ was achieved by 38.75 \% of water in a binder. Shear strength and P-wave velocity were measured using ISO/TS17892-7 and visualised as a function of compressive strength. The results show varying permeability and UCS of soil stabilised by varying ratio of CEM II/GGBS agents. 
	
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction} 
\label{sec:intro}

Soil stabilization and solidification (S/S) is used in many civil engineering works with aim to improve physical and mechanical properties and behaviour of subgrade soils \cite{Jauberthie,Lindh202102,ArabiWild,Lindh2004}. The improvement in the compression characteristics of soils is required prior to road or buildings constructions and other engineering earthworks. The quality of the stabilized soil strongly depends on the binder used for stabilization. The most common stabilizing agents are cement \cite{Rekik} and cementitious products, such as fly ash \cite{Tastan,Hasan} or cement kiln dust \cite{Solanki,Lindh2021a}. The by-products of industrial processes or iron and steel-making, such as ground granulated blast furnace slag \cite{Mozejko} and sludge \cite{Ingunza,Sharif} are also used as binders. Besides the type of the stabilising agent, the process of soil stabilization includes practical estimation and determining the amount of admixtures mixed with the tested soil affects its compressive strength and microstructure \cite{Lindh2001,Ahmed}.

Soil compactness, permeability and strength are important technical properties of the soil that should be measured before the construction works \cite{LindhWinter,XU2021114774}. Compactness of soil is reflected in its structure, which includes the morphometric parameters of size, shape, and arrangement of elements. Evaluation of soil compactness is related to measurement of shape indices of pores and solid phase elements along with the physical soil parameters \cite{Bryk}. Specifically, compacted clays are one of the most frequently used materials in the infrastructure construction due to their physical and mechanical properties, because compacted clays, usually stay in an unsaturated condition \cite{Zou}. 

Extensive literature is available on S/S of soil and measuring its properties including reports on soil types, methods of testing, and results of stabilisation \cite{Anagnostopoulos,Kallenetal2016,Lemenkov2021a,HUANG2022115452}.

\section{Methodology}
Evaluation of the soil properties is based on various criteria which include regional type of soils, ISO standard requirements for workflow, amount and availability of dredged samples. The samples were collected from the site prepared for construction of the SCA Biorefinery Östrand AB in coastal region of Timrå municipality, Bothnian Bay. Using the materials and technical guidance enables to determine the type and amount of binder that should be used for a particular case as a the stabilising agent. Methodology included a series of geotechnical tests performed in the Swedish Geotechnical Institute (SGI). In this study we used the guidance of Swedish Institute for Standards for the workflow on soil testing \cite{SIS2017,SIS2018,SIS2019}. 

\subsection{Experimental design Phase 1}
The stabilisation of soil followed the existing general procedure requirements developed by the Swedish Institute for Standards \cite{SIS2018}. To optimise the experimental design, a special methodology was used based on the statistical experimental planning and Swedish Geotechnical Institute. An experimental design of mixture procedure was used for the selection of optimal ratio of the stabilising agent (cement and slag in tested varied amounts) and water. The mixture procedure consisted of preparation of various ratios of water and soil with experimentally varying quantities and/or types of stabilising agents (cement and slag) and testing the strength, compactness and permeability of the resulting mixtures. Thus, in the experimental design, three different binder combinations were evaluated with changed ratio, Figure 1. The ratio between the stabilising agents (cement and slag) in the three selected recipes was 70/30\%, 50/50\% and 30/70\%, respectively. A series of tests has been performed on these three mixtures to determine the compaction characteristics and permeability of soil samples and to measure the uniaxial compressive strength (UCS).  The experiment included the factorial experiment with varying water ratio and amount of binder. In addition, the ultrasonic P-wave tests were conducted using the existing methodology \cite{Lindh202102} to determine the strength of soil and estimate its suitability for construction works.

In addition to binder combinations, the effect of a varied water ratio was tested together with different quantities of the stabilising agents (cement and slag) in various proportions in Phase 1 of the experiment. The experimental design with simultaneous evaluation of mixture proportions, binder quantities and water quotas helps to produce information that can be used to control the process in the field. the dredged masses are obtained because the water quotas are expected to vary.  

The combination of a high water ratio ($H_W$) and a low amount of binder ($L_B$) constitutes a “worst case scenario” as the material risks having a higher permeability and lower technical strength. The scenario will henceforth be referred to as "high water low binder" ($H_WL_B$). The opposite, low water ratio and high amount of binder gives “the best case, ie a material with low permeability and high technical strength. This combination is further referred to in the text as "low water high binder" ($L_WH_B$).

In Figure 4, the best and worst combination is marked in a matrix based on factor experiments (ie two variables that are tested at two levels, in the figure mentioned as "high" and "low"). High and low water ratios in these experiments consisted of 190\% and 139\%, respectively. High and low amount of binder consisted of 120 and 150 kg binder per $m^3$ of the mud mass, respectively.

\subsection{Experimental design Phase 2}

After an initial evaluation of phase 1, a coarse selection of suitable recipes was obtained which was further optimized in phase 2. Phase 1 resulted in 30\% cement and 70\% slag constituting appropriate proportions at both 120 and 150 kg binder volume per cubic meter of dredged material. In phase 2, an additional amount of binder was added: 100 kg of binder per cubic meter of dredged material. The reduction was based on the established requirements with regard to strength and permeability being met with a binder with an amount of binder of 120 kg per cubic meter of dredged material. The goal of phase 2 was thus to be able to optimize the cost of the recipe without losing technical / environmental technical quality. Cost optimization of the recipe also provides optimization of reduced carbon footprint as e.g. the amount of binder is driving this type of environmental impact. The performed test combinations in phase 2 are reported in Figure 5.

Graph in Figure \ref{fig:05} shows the experimental setups Phase 1 and 2. Phase 1 is indicated by blue dots and phase 2 by red dots. Blue circle shows where Phase 1 and 2 overlap. High and low water ratios in these experiments consisted of 190 resp. 139\%. High, low and extra low amount of binder consisted of 150, 120 resp. 100 kg / m3. Compared with experimental setup 1, the minimum amount of binder has been reduced to 100 kg per cubic meter of dredged material. 

Furthermore, experiments were carried out to assess the possibilities of using green liquor sludge and ash from SCA's plant as landfill. The experiments performed were density determination according to heavy laboratory packing (modified Proctor). CRS trials were rejected as the material was judged to be too solid to obtain any reasonable results. This assessment was based on performed Proctor tests on current material (see Appendix 1). However, the result depends on the composition of the material and can vary with the degree of carbonation of the ash and the water ratio of the green liquor sludge.

\subsection{Soil permeability}
As in the lab study, measurement of soil permeability was performed using the existing methodology of Swedish Institute for Standards \cite{SIS2019}. The workflow included a series of tests according to the cell pressure permeate method (SGI method, edition 15). Within each group, the permeability decreases with increasing amount of binder, see Figure 1. The test is performed with double samples and the spread within each group seems to decrease with increasing amount of binder. 

The permeability tests were performed according to the cell pressure permeate method (SGI method edition 15). The specimens tested in phase 1 came from the worst-case scenario, ie high water ratio and low amount of binder. Despite this, the results showed low to very low permeability, see Figure 23. Samples with an increased slag content gave a denser material. However, the quality requirement of 10-8 m/s was only achieved with 30\% of cement and 70\% of slag. Only samples from the $H_WL_B$ series were included in the experiments. 

In laboratory Phase 2, eight permeability tests were performed. The recipe consisted of 30\% cement and 70\% slag at two different amounts of binder and two different water quotas. The results are shown in Table 6. The quality requirement of 10-8 m / s was achieved for low water ratio and low binder quantity (sample 1), high water ratio and high binder quantity (sample 4) and low / high water ratio and high binder quantity (samples 6 and 8). 

The response area from the analysis is reported in Figure \ref{fig:xx2b} (2D and 3D, respectively). In the yellow-red areas, a significant interaction is shown between a change in the amount of binder and the water ratio. The interaction results in a sharp deterioration in technical and environmental technical quality through increased permeability from about 10-7 to 10-8 m/s. The strong interaction shows the importance of working with well-thought-out recipe optimisation at the lab level if the goal is to be able to control the mixing process in the field so that set quality requirements are achieved and project uncertainties are reduced. A robust recipe must contain 120 kg of binder per cubic meter of dredged material to provide sufficiently low permeability at several different water quotas.

Results from the permeability tests for the different materials and different binder levels. The duplicate experiments have been performed on all the samples. The distribution of values within each recipe mixture demonstrates the decrease of permeability which  gradually reduces values along with added slag and reduced cement, and vice versa, see Figure \ref{fig:xx2b}, right.

\subsection{Proctor compaction test}
The Proctor compaction tests were performed on a mixture of ash and green liquor sludge in order to determine optimal moisture content at which soil becomes the most dense and achieves maximum dry density. Proctor tests were performed on a mixture of ash and green liquor sludge. The mutual ratio was 50/50 calculated on the dry material. The Proctor tests are performed according to SS027109. The optimal water ratio is about 39\% to achieve the best packing of the material, i.e. the highest dry density, see Figure \ref{fig:26}. The maximal dry density at 1,12 $g/cm^3$ was achieved by 38.75 \% of water in a binder.

\subsection{Measurement of strength and P and flex wave velocity}

The specimens have a diameter of about 50 mm and a height of about 100 mm. The strength is determined according to ISO / TS 17892-7 \cite{SIS2017} according to the rules of the Swedish Institute for Standards. The deformation rate was about 1\% / min which gives an approximate speed of 1 mm / min at this specimen size. Results from the uniaxial compression tests are presented in Figure 1. The shear strength of the test specimens is obtained by multiplying the compressive strength by 0.5. All recipes meet the requirements for a minimum compressive strength of 280 kPa, compared with the laboratory study.

The results of the uniaxial pressure tests for each mixture are shown in Figure \ref{fig:30}. The result must be multiplied by 0.5 to obtain shear strength. \section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

The compressibility and strength of clayey soil are a basic frame for interpreting their natural characteristics and suitability for construction. The physical and mechanical properties of soils depend on the inherent natural soil structure, that is, fabric and bonding characteristics \cite{Burland}. However, after stabilisation, these properties may be improved towards a higher strength and compressibility which enables using soil for construction. The quality of soils stabilized by cement/slag agents can be assessed using methods similar to those for compacted soils. The compaction properties of the soil in the sampled specimens were identified using Proctor compaction tests. The relationship between the water ratio in the pores of soil and dry density in were explored using statistical plotting.
