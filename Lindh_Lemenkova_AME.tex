\begin{abstract}
  \label{abstract}

	Measuring permeability, compressive strength and compactness of the stabilised soil is important for engineering construction in the coastal area of Östrand in the Bothnian Bay of the Baltic Sea with dominated clayey soils. To gain insights into the variations of permeability and uniaxial compressive strength (UCS) of soils stabilised by different ratio of binders, soil samples were used as a test material stabilised by different ratio of the stabilised agents and varying water content. The binder agents included cement (Basement CEM II / A-V, SS EN 197-1) and ground-granulated blast furnace slag (GGBS). The samples were collected from the coastal region of Timrå, Bothnian Bay. The aim was to estimate the compressive characteristics, permeability and compactness of soil by measuring Uniaxial Compressive Strength (UCS) for each of admixtures. Factorial experiment was sued to test water ratio and amount of binder as variables. Permeability tests were performed on the stabilised soil with varied stabilising agents in binder ratio $H_WL_B$ with ratio 70/30\%, 50/50\%, and 30/70\%. The highest level of permeability was achieved by 70/30\% ratio of cement/slag, while the lowest -- by 30/70\%. The Proctor compaction tests were performed on a mixture of ash and green liquor sludge in order to determine optimal moisture content at which soil becomes the most dense and achieves maximum dry density. The maximal dry density at 1,12 $g/cm^3$ was achieved by 38.75 \% of water in a binder. Shear strength and P-wave velocity were measured using ISO/TS17892-7 and visualised as a function of compressive strength. The results show varying permeability and UCS of soil stabilised by varying ratio of CEM II/GGBS agents. 
	
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction} 
\label{sec:intro}

Soil stabilization and solidification (S/S) is used in many civil engineering works with aim to improve physical and mechanical properties and behaviour of subgrade soils \cite{Jauberthie,Lindh202102,ArabiWild,Lindh2004}. The improvement in the compression characteristics of soils is required prior to road or buildings constructions and other engineering earthworks. The quality of the stabilized soil strongly depends on the binder used for stabilization. The most common stabilizing agents are cement \cite{Rekik} and cementitious products, such as fly ash \cite{Tastan,Hasan} or cement kiln dust \cite{Solanki,Lindh2021a}. The by-products of industrial processes or iron and steel-making, such as ground granulated blast furnace slag \cite{Mozejko} and sludge \cite{Ingunza,Sharif} are also used as binders. Besides the type of the stabilising agent, the process of soil stabilization includes practical estimation and determining the amount of admixtures mixed with the tested soil affects its compressive strength and microstructure \cite{Lindh2001,Ahmed}.

Soil compactness, permeability and strength are important technical properties of the soil that should be measured during S/S techniques before the construction works \cite{LindhWinter,XU2021114774}. Compactness of soil is reflected in its structure, which includes the morphometric parameters of size, shape, and arrangement of elements. Evaluation of soil compactness is related to measurement of shape indices of pores and solid phase elements along with the physical soil parameters \cite{Bryk}. Specifically, compacted clays are one of the most frequently used materials in the infrastructure construction due to their physical and mechanical properties, because compacted clays, usually stay in an unsaturated condition \cite{Zou}. 

Extensive literature is available on the S/S of soil and measuring its properties including reports on soil types, methods of testing, and results of stabilisation \cite{Anagnostopoulos,Kallenetal2016,Lemenkov2021a,HUANG2022115452}.

\section{Methodology}
Evaluation of the soil properties is based on various criteria which include regional type of soils, ISO standard requirements for workflow, amount and availability of dredged samples. The samples were collected from the site prepared for construction of the SCA Biorefinery Östrand AB in coastal region of Timrå municipality, Bothnian Bay, Figure \ref{fig:01}. Using the materials and technical guidance enables to determine the type and amount of binder that should be used for a particular case as a the stabilising agent. Methodology included a series of soil tests performed in the Swedish Geotechnical Institute (SGI). The documents of the Swedish Institute for Standards were sued as guidance for the workflow \cite{SIS2017,SIS2018,SIS2019}.

\subsection{Experimental design Phase 1}
The stabilisation of soil followed the existing general procedure requirements developed by the Swedish Institute for Standards \cite{SIS2018}. To optimise the experimental design, a special methodology was used based on the statistical experimental planning and Swedish Geotechnical Institute. An experimental design of mixture procedure was used for the selection of optimal ratio of the stabilising agent (cement and slag in tested varied amounts) and water. The mixture procedure consisted of preparation of various ratios of water and soil with experimentally varying quantities and/or types of stabilising agents (cement and slag) and testing the strength, compactness and permeability of the resulting mixtures.

In the experimental design, three different binder combinations were evaluated with changed ratio, Figure 1. The ratio between the stabilising agents (cement and slag) in the three selected recipes was 70/30\%, 50/50\% and 30/70\%, respectively. A series of tests has been performed on these three mixtures to determine the compaction characteristics and permeability of soil samples and to measure the Uniaxial Compressive Strength (UCS).  The experiment included the factorial experiment with varying water ratio and amount of binder. In addition, the ultrasonic P-wave tests were conducted using the existing methodology \cite{Lindh202102} to determine the strength of soil and estimate its suitability for construction works. 

In addition to binder combinations, the effect of a varied water ratio was tested together with different quantities of the stabilising agents (cement and slag) in various proportions in Phase 1 of the experiment. The experimental design with simultaneous evaluation of mixture proportions, binder quantities and water quotas helps to produce information that can be used to control the process in the field. the dredged masses are obtained because the water quotas are expected to vary.  

The combination of a high water ratio ($H_W$) and a low amount of binder ($L_B$) constitutes a “worst case scenario” as the material risks having a higher permeability and lower technical strength. The scenario will henceforth be referred to as "high water low binder" ($H_WL_B$). The opposite, low water ratio and high amount of binder gives “the best case, ie a material with low permeability and high technical strength. This combination is further referred to in the text as "low water high binder" ($L_WH_B$).

In Figure 4, the best and worst combination is marked in a matrix based on factor experiments (ie two variables that are tested at two levels, in the figure mentioned as "high" and "low"). High and low water ratios in these experiments consisted of 190\% and 139\%, respectively.

\subsection{Experimental design Phase 2}

After an initial evaluation of Phase 1, a coarse selection of suitable recipes was obtained which was further optimized in Phase 2. Phase 1 resulted in 30\% cement and 70\% slag constituting appropriate proportions at both 120 and 150 kg binder volume per cubic meter of dredged material. In Phase 2, an additional amount of binder was added: 100 kg of binder per cubic meter of dredged material. 

The reduction was based on the established requirements with regard to strength and permeability being met with a binder with an amount of binder of 120 kg per cubic meter of dredged material. The goal of Phase 2 was thus to be able to optimize the cost of the recipe without losing technical / environmental technical quality. Cost optimisation of the recipe also provides the optimisation of the reduced carbon footprint as e.g. the amount of binder is driving this type of environmental impact. The performed test combinations in Phase 2 are reported in Figure 5.

Graph in Figure \ref{fig:03} shows the experimental setups Phase 1 and 2. The Phase 1 is indicated by blue dots and Phase 2 by - red dots, respectively. Blue circle shows where Phase 1 and 2 overlap. High and low water ratios in these experiments consisted of 190 respectively 139\%. High, low and extra low amount of binder consisted of 150, 120 respectively, for $100 kg/m^3$. Compared with the experimental setup 1, the minimum amount of binder has been reduced to $100 kg/m^3$ of the dredged material. 

Furthermore, the experiments were carried out to assess the possibilities of using green liquor sludge and ash from SCA's plant as landfill. The experiments performed were density determination according to heavy laboratory packing (modified Proctor test). CRS trials were rejected as the material was judged to be too solid to obtain any reasonable results. This assessment was based on performed Proctor tests on current material. However, the result depends on the composition of the material and can vary with the degree of carbonation of the ash and the water ratio of the green liquor sludge.

\subsection{Soil permeability}
As in the lab study, measurement of soil permeability was performed using the existing methodology of Swedish Institute for Standards \cite{SIS2019}. The workflow included a series of tests according to the cell pressure permeate method (SGI method, edition 15). Within each group, the permeability decreases with increasing amount of binder, see Figure 1. The test is performed with double samples and the spread within each group seems to decrease with increasing amount of binder. 

The permeability tests were performed according to the cell pressure permeate method (SGI method edition 15). The specimens tested in phase 1 came from the worst-case scenario, ie high water ratio and low amount of binder. Despite this, the results showed low to very low permeability, see Figure 23. Samples with an increased slag content gave a denser material. However, the quality requirement of 10-8 m/s was only achieved with 30\% of cement and 70\% of slag. Only samples from the $H_WL_B$ series were included in the experiments.

\subsection{Proctor compaction test}
The Proctor compaction tests were performed on a mixture of ash and green liquor sludge in order to determine optimal moisture content at which soil becomes the most dense and achieves maximum dry density. Proctor tests were performed on a mixture of ash and green liquor sludge. The mutual ratio was 50/50 calculated on the dry material. The Proctor tests are performed according to SS027109. The optimal water ratio is about 39\% to achieve the best packing of the material, i.e. the highest dry density, see Figure \ref{fig:26}. The maximal dry density at 1,12 $g/cm^3$ was achieved by 38.75 \% of water in a binder. 

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{Fig_26}
  \caption{Results from the Proctor compaction tests of green liquor sludge and ash. $W_{opt} \sim 39\%$.}
  \label{fig:26}
\end{figure}

The relationship of water content in a binder vs. dry density of soil is presented in Figure \ref{fig:26}. The data were tested using Least Square method, Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) algorithm. The results of the test are presented in Figure \ref{fig:26} (Proctor compaction test) 
as an example of the soil compaction vs water content relationship obtained for the tested soil samples. 

The changes in the maximal dry density depending on water content in a binder indicated that wetness of soil processed by green liquor sludge and ash could be used to predict variation of compactness with changed ratio of water in the diapason of 30\%-65\% (Figure 8). The maximal peak of dry density was achieved by water content of 38-39\% in the soil masses. After that the soil dry density decreased exponentially along with the incraese of water content in the soil until 65\% (maximally measured value). Hence, the results of the Proctor tests demonstrated that dry density in soil is sensitive to changes in soil wetness.

\subsection{Measurement of strength and P and flex wave velocity}

Nondestructive testing was performed by ultrasonic P-waves to measure soil strength after stabilisation. The use of the applied geophysical methods using P-waves is a modern approach to analyse strength of the S/S soils. It provides a rapid, robust and reliable approach to analyse the stabilized soils, alternative to the existing traditional methods. The specimens had a diameter of ca. 50 mm and a height of ca. 100 mm. The strength is determined according to ISO / TS 17892-7 \cite{SIS2017} according to the rules of the Swedish Institute for Standards. The deformation rate was about 1\% / min which gives an approximate speed of 1 mm / min at this specimen size. The results from the UCS tests are presented in Figure 1. The shear strength of the test specimens is obtained by multiplying the compressive strength by 0.5. All recipes meet the requirements for a minimum compressive strength of 280 kPa, compared with the laboratory study.

Therefore, the experiments have been performed for each recipe and the values of the compressibility and permeability were recorded for each binder combination. Besides, the Uniaxial Compressive Strength (UCS) and P-wave velocities were recorded for materials taken from the sample sites A4 and B4 with 120 and 150 kg of binder per $m^3$ of the dredged material. 

The graphs have been visualised as statistical plots for each mixture in 4 cases (see Figures 9 and 10). Arendal 2, Gothenburg and Kolkajen, Stockholm. A certain displacement can be discerned, that is, slightly lower strength at the same P-wave velocity for the scaled-up experiment in Phase 3. The results are in general consistent with the expected variation based on the comparable similar geotechnical surveys of soil.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

A series of tests were performed to determine the properties of the stabilized soils collected from the selected sampling sites in coastal area of Timrå municipality, Bothnian Bay, Baltic Sea, northern Sweden. The collected soil samples were obtained from various tests of the Östrand AB (Figure \ref{fig:01}). Samples were processed, prepared and stabilized in the laboratory of SGI, Gothenburg. Measurements of the physical-mechanical properties of soil was performed according to the technical standards of SIS. The study aimed to assess the effects of various binder combinations on the compressive strength, compactness and permeability of soils. Soil samples were stabilised by the two agents: cement (Basement CEM II / A-V) and slag (GGBFS). The amount of binders were changed to evaluate optimal ratio of binder and water for soil stabilisation. 

The velocities of the ultrasonic P-wave propagation in soil masses were tested to evaluate the strength of the stabilised soil samples. P-wave velocities were determined on specimens of a dredged silt loamy soil stabilized with mixtures of two agents (CEM II / A-V and GGBS) following technical standards SGI and workflow requirements of SIS for testing procedures. A grain distribution analysis was performed on the aggregated samples. Compression strength, compactness and permeability of soil were also determined using UCS tests, SGI method for testing permeability (edition 15) and modified Proctor compaction test, respectively. The compaction properties of soil in tested specimens, identified using modified Proctor compaction tests, demonstrated the relationship between the water ratio in the pores of soil and dry density, which was demonstrated on statistical graphs.

A comparison of the UCS in soil samples stabilised by various proportions of binder was performed. Measurements of the ultrasonic P-waves velocity  demonstrated to be a fast, robust and reliable non-destructive method of evaluating soil strength. Compared to the traditional destructive methods, applied geophysical testing methods can applied used to measure physical and mechanical characteristics of the stabilized soil samples in a more flexible and simple way. Since measuring P-wave velocity is a non-destructive geophysical approach, it has the advantage of test replication. Thus, the repeatability of P-wave testing reduced the number of tests within the set of experiments and optimises workflow. In this way, mixture design can be tested through adding of binders in various proportions, to determine properties and bearing capacity of the stabilized soil. Considering these advantages, the P-waves velocities were measured to evaluate the soil strength. 

Changes in ultrasonic P-waves velocity were evaluated for each type of the stabilising agent and demonstrated in graphs showing P-waves and UCS of the samples stabilised by binders in various ratio (Figure 11). The quality of soils stabilized by cement/slag agents was assessed using methods similar to those for compacted soils. The P-wave velocities of the stabilized soil samples demonstrated the general increase along with increasing strength of the samples over time of curing. Herewith, higher velocities were recorded for the soils stabilised by binder proportion cement/slag as 30/70, while lower velocities were obtained for soils stabilised by binder proportion cement/slag as 30/70, respectively. 

The velocity of P-waves increased with increasing strength of the soils and time of curing. The increase in P-wave velocity with strength was more pronounced for the case of B4 testing where 150 kg/m3 of binder was used for soil samples compared to the test A4 with 120 kg of binder, which demonstrated the direct effects of binder admixture on the overall increase of soil strength. The transmission of the P-wave occurs along the fastest path in the structure of the soil mass, which is directly related to the strength and stiffness of the soil mass stabilised by the agents (cement/slag). Hence, samples of the stabilised soil with $H_BL_W$ content demonstrated higher strength and increased velocity of P-waves compared to the samples with $H_WL_B$  content.

The compressibility and strength of clayey soil are a basic frame for interpreting their natural characteristics and suitability for construction. The physical and mechanical properties of soils depend on the inherent natural soil structure, that is, fabric and bonding characteristics \cite{Burland}. However, after stabilisation, these properties may be improved towards a higher strength and compressibility which enables using soil for construction. Stabilising binders (cement and slag) react with water and show a bonding effect that produced an increase in compressive strength of the stabilised soil samples. In such a way, the stiffness and durability of the soil-binder system is achieved, because the initial natural voids and pores of the raw soil structure are filled by the stabilising agents which increase strength of the stabilised soil, which was demonstrated in the obtained results. 

Admixture of stabilising agents, such as cement and slag in various proportions, demonstrated increase in soil strength. Related to the industrial goals, stabilisation of soil leads to the overall increase of bearing capacity of the soil structure, which was demonstrated in the performed experiments. A case study of this work included a coastal area of Östrand in the Bothnian Bay of the Baltic Sea, northern Sweden, where engineering constructions are planned. The dominated soil type of the dredged materials included silt loams. The results of the study can be considered in similar works with clayey soil evaluated for construction works. Future research can include further experiments on changing binder ratio and stabilising agents and to develop criteria of binder/water proportions specifically for S/S treatment of clayey soils collected in the coastal areas of northern Sweden.
